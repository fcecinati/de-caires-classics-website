The story

What the carpets
were hiding

A good deal on a tidy-looking 912. Then the interior came out.

It arrived red, the colour it had worn since New York: trimmed inside, carpets down, underside painted, nothing to alarm anyone standing back from it. That impression lasted exactly as long as it took to start taking things out.

Underneath, the shell had rusted down. Roughly the lower half of the car would have to be remade, not repaired but remade. The engine was seized with a failed piston, and the three-piece alloy crankcase everything else bolts to was cracked. The original wiring loom was found burnt through, cooked by its own charging feed. Half the cooling tinware was simply missing.

Honestly, it was a job large enough that selling the project on was, for a moment, the reasonable option. It was kept instead. A jig was built first, so that every suspension point sat exactly where Porsche's blueprints said it should, and only then did the cutting begin. The upper body survived. From the waist down, most of this car is metal that did not exist in 2019, much of it formed by hand in an English workshop.

Seven years later the crankcase has been specialist-welded and re-plumbed for better oil circulation, and the engine around it is 2.0 litres instead of 1.6 and almost entirely new: CNC-machined heads, racing pistons, Carrillo rods, twin Weber 44 IDFs. The capacity is the point: size buys torque low down where a wilder camshaft would only buy revs, so the car pulls from little and stays docile in traffic. 2.0 litres is also what Porsche gave the 911 of 1965, here with two fewer cylinders behind the rear axle. The gearbox was stripped to the last bearing and built again, and the loom made from nothing, one wire at a time. Under flared arches and a ducktail, the paint is Crystal Blue, a Porsche special-order colour.

The car outside didn't look that bad. But when I started taking things out, I found what they were hiding. It was really bad.

Umberto De Caires

Six angles
for a feature

  1. One pair of hands. Fabrication, engine, gearbox, loom, trim decisions: almost all of it done by one man over seven years.
  2. A jig, then a shell. Built from Porsche's own blueprints so that every suspension point sat true before a single panel was cut away. Roughly 30 percent of the replacement metal was then made by hand.
  3. The case that should have ended it. A cracked three-piece crankcase is where most of these engines stop. This one was welded by specialists and its oil circulation redesigned, so the cause went with the symptom.
  4. The 912 argument. Less weight behind the rear axle, sharper balance. The case for the cheap Porsche being the better-handling one.
  5. Parts that no longer exist. Unobtainable small fittings, modelled and 3D-printed in the workshop, then finished to match.
  6. The next dream. To 3D-scan a 1950s Formula 1 car in a museum and build one from scratch, because a car like that cannot be bought, only made.

Three details worth a paragraph

Honesty

A new plate, so the old one isn't lying

The original Karmann body plate carries Farbton 6608. The car is now Farbton 6825. Rather than leave a plate that contradicted the car, a new one was made to the factory pattern, same body number, new paint number, and the original kept with the car.

Resourcefulness

Headlamps borrowed from a 356

The clear-glass lamps he wanted are no longer made, and the standard 912 lenses were not to his taste. So 356 units were modified to fit the 912's holders: clear glass, the pattern set inside, exactly the look intended.

Foresight

Spare wires, run before the loom went in

The burnt original was replaced by a loom built from nothing, one wire at a time. Unused wires were run inside the car while it was open, so anything added later can be fed cleanly rather than taped along the outside.

The signature

Umberto De Caires

He grew up in his father's workshop, an aerospace engineer's bench where engines and mechanisms were taken apart until they were understood. He trained as a mechanic in Venezuela on a course built around racing, where tolerances matter and shortcuts cost races, moved into custom motorcycles, then to Italy for bodywork and paint. England came next: years of independent work on Jaguar, Porsche, Alfa Romeo, Maserati and Aston Martin. 912R was built in that English workshop, alone, over seven years.

Last month he left England for Gavorrano, in the Maremma, Italy, where De Caires Classics is opening its own garage. 912R is the car that closes one chapter and opens the next.
