Foreword

A car worth explaining

This is the complete account of a single restoration: what the car was, what was found inside it, and every decision taken to bring it back. It is written to be read from the first page, and kept as a record of the work.

Some cars are simply serviced and sold. This was not one of them. What began as a fair deal on a rough but promising 912 became a near-total rebuild, most of it carried out by one pair of hands. The pages that follow tell that story in order, and then take the car apart system by system so that nothing is left unsaid. Where a fact is still to be confirmed from the car's documents, it is marked to confirm rather than guessed.

01 · The car

Why a 912

The badge first, the bargain second, and a rebuild no one had bargained for.

It began with the badge. A love of the Porsche marque, and a 912 that came up at a good price: on paper, a good deal. The car did not look bad from the outside. It was only later, once it was stripped, that the true size of the job revealed itself, and a fair deal turned into a very large one.

The 912 was Porsche's more attainable car, built so that a driver who could not stretch to a 911 could still stay with the marque. It ran from 1965 to 1968, with a final fuel-injected 912E in 1969, after which the model was dropped and the factory's attention went wholly to the 911. That short life is why the car is rare today.

Mechanically it shares the 911's floorpan and body; the interior is much the same too. The difference is behind the rear axle, where a flat-four sits in place of the flat-six. Less weight hung out at the back changes the whole feel of the car. It is not as fast as a 911. To the people who drive them, it handles better for exactly that reason: a different sensation from the same car.

I always loved the Porsche brand. The 912 handles completely differently, less weight in the back. It is a different feeling of the same car.

02 · Condition on arrival

What the carpets were hiding

Sound to the eye, and almost gone beneath.

The car flattered to deceive. The interior was in place, carpets and all, and the underside had been painted, so at a glance it looked like a manageable project. That impression lasted precisely as long as it took to start taking things out.

Underneath, the shell had rusted down. Roughly the lower half of the car would have to be remade. It was, honestly, a job large enough that at one point the reasonable option was to sell the project on rather than see it through. It was kept. The compensation, beyond the finished car, was a deep and lasting understanding of how these bodies are built, knowledge that carries straight across to the 911, since the two share the same structure.

  • Presented well: trimmed interior in place, underside painted, no obvious alarm from outside.
  • Reality once stripped: corrosion through roughly the bottom half of the shell.
  • Upper body: largely sound and saved.
  • Lower body: substantially gone, requiring fabrication rather than repair.
  • Engine: seized, with a failed piston (covered in full later).
  • Air ducting: many of the cooling tin panels that direct air over the engine were missing.
  • Wiring: the original loom found burnt through.

The car outside didn't look that bad. But when I started taking things out, I found what they were hiding. It was really bad.

03 · Structure and body

Made true again

First a jig to hold the car honest, then most of a new shell built around it.

The first task was not to cut, but to hold. A jig was built so that every suspension point sat exactly where Porsche intended, using the factory blueprints as the reference. With the car locked to a structure known to be true, the new metal could be formed around it with confidence. Only then did the cutting begin. The upper body survived; from the waist down, most of the shell was remade.

Panel by panel

Roughly seventy percent of the replacement panels were bought in. The remainder were fabricated by hand, and throughout, as much original metal was saved as could honestly be kept. Where weight could be removed, it was: several outer panels are carbon fibre, reinforced with glassfibre, a deliberate saving on a car that was never going to be the most powerful on the road.

A racer's detail: the fuel filler

The filler flap was deleted from the wing, and the wing made without it. The filling neck was relocated forward, up by the bonnet, purely because it belongs there: a competition touch on a road car.

I made the jig from the blueprints, and I tried to save as much as I could of the original car.

04 · Colour

A blue that had to be asked for

The blue is a genuine Porsche factory colour, not an invention. It is not strictly correct to a 1965 car: this is an early example, and the shade entered the catalogue roughly two years later. But it remains a period 912 colour, and it is exactly the kind of colour that, then as now, was a paid-for special order rather than a standard choice. That small extravagance suits the car.

05 · The engine

Two litres, and almost entirely new

A seized, cracked motor became the excuse to build a better one.

The engine arrived seized. A piston had let go, and with it the cylinders and heads were beyond use. Worse, the three-piece aluminium crankcase, the housing on which everything else is built, was cracked. This is a known weakness on these units, related to how the oil circulates. The case was sent away to be welded by specialists, the sort of meticulous work that must be done exactly right or not at all, and the oil circulation was improved to address the cause rather than only the symptom.

Faced with rebuilding so much of it in any case, the decision was to build something better than standard. Capacity rose from 1.6 to 2.0 litres. Nearly everything inside is new; the single significant survivor is the crankshaft, which was sound, and was balanced and kept.

06 · Cooling, fuelling and exhaust

Built for the road it will actually meet

Choices made for a car that now has to sit in traffic these engines never anticipated.

  • Exhaust: stainless steel throughout.
  • Fuel pump: the original mechanical pump, never the most reliable part, removed in favour of an electric pump.
  • Fuel filter and regulator: a Malpassi filter with an integral adjustable pressure regulator, so fuel pressure can be set precisely.
  • Air filters: two new K&N filters.
  • Primary oil cooling: an aluminium oil cooler.
  • Secondary oil cooling: an additional remote oil cooler with its own electric fan, added specifically so the oil stays cool in the modern traffic these cars were never designed to sit in.

Why a second cooler

These cars were built for open roads, not for crawling in queues for hours. The remote cooler and fan are an honest concession to how the car will be used today, there to keep the oil temperature down and protect the freshly built engine.

07 · Running gear

Everything off, everything through

Suspension, brakes and gearbox, each taken fully apart and rebuilt.

The whole car was taken down. Every suspension component was stripped, then replaced or refinished, powder-coated, and reassembled. Some original parts earned their place back; where an item was worn, or simply prudent to renew, it was renewed.

The gearbox

The gearbox was completely rebuilt. The good gears were retained; the worn ones, reverse among them, were replaced. On principle, every bearing was renewed, even those that looked serviceable, because with the box already apart there was no sense in trusting an old bearing back into a fresh build.

  • Rebuild: stripped, cleaned, and reassembled completely.
  • Gears replaced: the worn gears, including reverse.
  • Gears retained: those found to be good.
  • Bearings: all renewed, without exception.
  • Fuel tank: new, with a new sender.

08 · Wheels and tyres

A little wider, for the way it drives

The car arrived on a mismatched set, two of them oversized. A new set was bought in its place. The tyres are marginally wider than standard and on a lower profile (a 55 section in place of the original 60), which keeps them close to the original look while adding grip. The original rear tyres were narrow; a slightly wider tyre at the back improves the handling, which was the point. That they also look right is a welcome bonus.

09 · Interior

Blue, orange, and Scottish tweed

Where the car stops being a restoration and becomes a personal statement.

If the body stays close to what Porsche made, the interior is where the car becomes personal. The starting point was the blue. From there came a Scottish tweed whose orange and blue sat perfectly against it, and an orange Alcantara chosen to match the tweed. The pairing carries an unspoken echo of the most famous racing livery in blue and orange, arrived at, by the builder's own account, mostly because the colours simply looked right together.

The seats were trimmed by a trusted upholsterer. The Alcantara chosen is not, strictly, the ideal grade for seats and leaves a slight crease, but it was the right orange, and the result was worth it.

Seats and safety

In place of a roll cage, which would have made a track car of something never meant to be one, there is a discreet reinforcement bar, cut in and added, with a four-point harness fixed to it. The two rear seats, the tiny ones that fit no adult and were only ever meant for children, were remade in the same materials.

Floor and sound

There is no carpet. The blue floor is left deliberately visible, dressed with aluminium mats painted blue to match. Hidden beneath sits Dynamat sound-deadening for the noise and vibration an aluminium-and-steel car will make. The front footwells are lined with aluminium panels and covered in a black diamond-pattern vinyl, so everything the eye reaches is tidy.

Dashboard and instruments

A new lower dashboard section was made, with a band of the same tweed running across it around the ignition and the lighter, in place of the original aluminium trim, simply because it looks better. This was a three-gauge car; two gauges were added, oil pressure and charging, the two worth watching.

The steering wheel

It arrived with an unattractive Momo wheel. An original was found but set aside as too large at 40cm. A 38cm was tried, and finally the current wheel chosen: a deep-dish four-spoke that brings the rim toward the driver for a more purposeful position, yet stays close in look to the original four-spoke, so the cabin keeps its period character.

Woodwork

Two small pieces of hardwood warm the whole: a gear knob, and a matching knob on the heater lever, cut from the same wood and to the same pattern.

10 · Glass, mirrors and rubbers

The parts you notice last

  • Windscreen: renewed. The original had begun to delaminate, a film creeping between its two layers, though it had not actually cracked.
  • Rear screen: original, saved and refitted.
  • Door and rear quarter glass: original, retained.
  • Rubbers: every rubber renewed. There is no old rubber anywhere on the car.
  • Frames: door glass frames and rear quarter window frames removed and powder-coated.
  • Mirror: a single mirror on the driver's side, where it is needed to overtake, a new part of the correct old type, on a car that arrived with none.

11 · Lights

A resourceful pair of headlights

The standard 912 headlights, with their large lenses, were not to the builder's taste, and the clear-glass lamps he wanted are no longer made. The solution was to take headlight units from a 356 and modify them to fit the 912's holders, giving the clear glass with the pattern set inside, exactly the look intended.

  • Headlights: 356 units modified to fit; clear glass, internal pattern.
  • Front indicators and surrounds: restored, frames powder-coated in their silver finish.
  • Rear lights: housing the stop, reverse and indicator functions; powder-coated, with new lenses and bulbs.

12 · Electrics

A new loom, wire by wire

The original loom came out burnt. The car was born with a generator, since converted to an alternator, and the main feed for the charging system, a thick wire running the whole length of the car, had cooked a run of the harness with it. Rather than chase a fault through scorched wiring, the loom was made again from nothing, by hand, one wire at a time.

A few spare wires were run deliberately and hidden inside, so any future addition can be made cleanly, without a cable taped along the outside looking like an afterthought. Switches that would clutter the dash, for the cooling fan and the hazards, are tucked out of sight to keep the fascia calm.

  • Loom: entirely remade by hand, wire by wire.
  • Charging: generator converted to alternator.
  • Provision: spare wires run internally for future additions (fuel pump, fan, and more).
  • Switchgear: a different hazard switch; fan switch hidden; dash kept clean.

13 · The engine bay, finished

Tidy where no one has to look

Because the cabin has no carpet, the firewall behind the rear seats is left visible from inside. Before the engine went back in, the engine-bay face of that firewall was insulated with Dynamat pads and then covered with a leather panel in a diamond pattern, hiding the deadening and making the bay read as tidily as the cabin. It is finishing applied where most builders would leave bare metal.

14 · Character and faithfulness

Far from standard, never modern

How far this car sits from the one that left the factory, honestly answered.

The honest answer is: a long way, and yet not at all. The engine and the interior are transformed. But the upgrades do not change how the car reads. The body is little altered beyond the rear flares, the slightly fuller tyres and the deck lid, and every one of those belongs to the vocabulary of period racing, nothing that was not done in the day.

The interior is the largest departure, and only in its colour; in spirit, the tweed and the palette are chosen in the idiom of the era. The dashboard is kept close to original, and the steering wheel, though different, stays a clear relative of the factory wheel. Nothing about the car feels like this decade. It carries the feeling of a 1960s and 1970s car, which was the whole intention. It will simply be faster.

Retained and changed

It's got the feeling of a 60s, 70s car, which is what I wanted. I never wanted it to look modern. It will be faster, though.

15 · The hardest parts

What the work asked for

Asked to name the most difficult and most satisfying part, the builder names two. The first is the structure: building the jig and getting every fabricated panel to sit exactly where it should. The second is the engine, so heavily modified that reassembling it and having it run cleanly was its own challenge. It ran well from the first time it started, which, given how much of it was new, is the quiet measure of the work.

  • The jig and panels: holding the car true and placing hand-made metal precisely.
  • The engine: assembling a heavily modified motor that ran well from the first start.

17 · For the next owner

A car to be understood

What the builder hopes for the car is simple, and worth saying plainly: someone who will take good care of it, who will understand everything that has been done, and who will enjoy it. And, one day, the chance to see it again at a show, in the hands of whoever comes next.

Ask about the next project and the answer is open. There are too many he would like to build, and it depends on what is possible. Pressed for a dream, it is a grand one: to 3D-scan a 1950s Formula 1 car in a museum and build it from scratch, because a car like that cannot be bought, only made. It is the kind of ambition that explains a car like this one.

I hope for someone who will take good care of it, understand everything that has been done, and just enjoy it.

Umberto
